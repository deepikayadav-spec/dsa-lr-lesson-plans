---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, students will be able to:

1. Recognise "I," "My," and "Myself" as signals to anchor the family tree at the speaker. *(UNDERSTANDING)*
2. Decode a relationship chain from the speaker's own viewpoint, one link at a time. *(APPLYING)*
3. Distinguish direction-sensitive phrasing (e.g. "son of daughter" vs. "husband of granddaughter"). *(ANALYZING)*
4. Evaluate relationships without assuming gender from a name. *(EVALUATING)*

## Hook (7–10 min)

Say: *"A girl looked at a photograph and said, 'I have no sisters or brothers, but the woman in the photo is my father's daughter.' Who is the woman in the photo?"*

Give 30 seconds, then solve together: *"My father's daughter — that's a description of a female child of the girl's father. The girl herself has no sisters, so she's her father's ONLY daughter. Which means 'my father's daughter' can only describe... herself."*

> *"That's today's whole trap — the question never says 'I am in the photo.' You have to work it out from the fact that there's no one else the description could possibly fit. Every first-person riddle today works the same way: anchor at the speaker, and rule out who else a phrase could mean."*

---

## Teaching Block A (10–19 min) — TEACH FROM NOTES BELOW

Covers: the anchoring principle and its three techniques, worked live.

**Beats to emphasise**

- **Core principle:** *"Whenever you see 'I said,' 'My mother,' 'My father's wife,' 'Myself' — pause. That's your signal to draw the speaker at the center of your diagram first, before anything else."*
- **Three techniques, write on the board:**
  1. **Anchor from the speaker** — literally start your sketch with the speaker in the middle.
  2. **Decode piece by piece** — same discipline as Session 19's symbolic chains, just in plain English now.
  3. **Direction matters** — "son of daughter" and "husband of granddaughter" are NOT interchangeable; work out exactly whose child or spouse is being described, in order.
- **Worked example, live (instructor-constructed, matching the source's described "mother-identification" example):** *"Pointing to a boy, Sunitha said, 'His mother is the only daughter of my mother.'"*
  1. Anchor: Sunitha is the speaker.
  2. "The only daughter of my mother" — Sunitha's mother's only daughter. If Sunitha herself is that only daughter, this phrase describes **Sunitha herself**.
  3. "His mother is [Sunitha]" → **the boy is Sunitha's son.**
- **Worked example, live (from the source, verified phrasing):** *"Pointing to a man, Kiran said, 'He is the husband of the granddaughter of my father.'"*
  1. Anchor: Kiran is the speaker.
  2. "The granddaughter of my father" — through Kiran, this is **Kiran's own daughter**.
  3. "He is the husband of [Kiran's daughter]" → **the man is Kiran's son-in-law.**

**Checkpoint (at 19 min)** — 10 s silent think, cold-call two students:
> *"Pointing to a boy, a woman said, 'He is the son of my sister.' Who is the boy to the woman?"*
> **Answer:** **Her nephew.**

## ⚡ ALS Activity 1 — Live Family Tree Build (19–25 min)

**ALS format:** Physical Demo / Kinesthetic Modeling — one student volunteer plays "the speaker" and stands at the front; as the instructor reads a riddle one clause at a time, additional volunteers step in and physically position themselves (with a labelled card: "father," "mother," "daughter," etc.) relative to the speaker, building a live human family tree. Chosen because anchoring at the speaker is the hardest habit to build from a written diagram alone — physically starting with one person at the center and adding others around them makes the anchoring instinct concrete.

**Setup line:**
> *"One volunteer, you're the speaker — stand at the front, center. I'll read a riddle one clause at a time. After each clause, a new volunteer steps in and stands in the correct relative position, holding a card with their relationship label."*

- Riddle, read clause by clause: *"Pointing to a girl, the speaker said... 'She is the daughter... of my father's only son.'"*
  1. Clause 1: *"my father's only son"* — pause. Ask the class: *"Who could this be?"* If the speaker has no brothers, this is **the speaker themself**. A volunteer holds up a "father's only son = ME" card and stands where the speaker already is (or right next to them, confirming it's the same person).
  2. Clause 2: *"She is the daughter of [the speaker]"* — a new volunteer steps in as "daughter," standing in the child position relative to the speaker.
  3. **Final answer: the girl is the speaker's own daughter.**

**How it surfaces:** Pause after every clause and ask the class to predict where the next volunteer should stand *before* they move — this forces the room to actively decode each clause instead of just watching.

**Debrief line:**
> *"Watch how the first clause resolved to 'the speaker themself' — that's the exact same trap as this session's Hook. Whenever a description could only be describing the speaker, position them right there in the center, don't invent a new person."*

**Cut rule:** If volunteers aren't available, do this with labelled cards moved by the instructor on the board instead of physical students — but keep the "one clause, pause, predict, reveal" rhythm intact.

## Teaching Block B (25–32 min) — TEACH FROM NOTES BELOW

Covers: common mistakes to avoid, and a second full worked example building complexity.

**Beats to emphasise**

- **Common mistakes, write on the board:**
  1. **Adopting the wrong perspective** — some riddles describe a photo, and it's tempting to anchor at the person *in* the photo instead of the speaker. Always anchor at whoever is *speaking*, not whoever is being looked at.
  2. **Assuming gender from a name** — a name alone never confirms gender in these puzzles; only an explicit relationship word (son, daughter, brother, sister, husband, wife) does.
  3. **Skipping relational links** — jumping straight from the first clue to a guessed final answer, instead of resolving every "of" one at a time.
- **Worked example, live, combining self-reference with a check:** *"A man says, 'She is the daughter of my father's only son.'"*
  1. "My father's only son" — if the man has no brothers, this is **himself**.
  2. "She is the daughter of [the man]" → **she is his own daughter.**
  3. *"Notice — same structure as Activity 1's riddle, but phrased slightly differently. Once you recognise the 'only son of my father' pattern always resolving to the speaker (when there are no brothers), you'll spot it instantly."*

**Checkpoint (at 32 min)** — cold-call:
> *"What's the very first thing you should identify in any first-person blood relations riddle, before decoding anything else?"*
> **Answer:** **Who the speaker is, and anchor the diagram at them** — not the person being described or pointed at.

## ⚡ ALS Activity 2 — Silent Solve → Vote-Lock → Reveal: Self-Reference Trap (32–40 min)

**ALS format:** Silent Solve, Vote-Lock, Then Reveal — students solve a fresh self-referencing riddle entirely on their own before the reveal. Deliberately different register from Activity 1's loud physical demo (quiet, individual, single big reveal), and directly tests the "only son/daughter of my parent = the speaker themself" pattern from Teaching Block B without any hand-holding.

**Setup line:**
> *"On your own, two minutes. 'Pointing to a photograph, a man said, "She is the daughter of my mother's only son."' Who is the woman in the photo, to the man? Write your answer and your reasoning, hold it up when I say show."*

Give 2 minutes silent work, then: *"Show me — three, two, one, show."*

**The reveal, step by step:**
1. "My mother's only son" — if the man is his mother's only son (no brothers), this describes **the man himself**.
2. "She is the daughter of [the man]" → **the woman is the man's own daughter.**

**Debrief line:**
> *"If you got this in under thirty seconds, that's exactly the instinct we're building — 'only son of my mother' or 'only daughter of my father' should now trigger 'that's the speaker' almost automatically, the same way you now instantly know A×B means father."*

**Cut rule:** If running short, cut the silent window to 90 seconds but always show the full two-step reasoning in the reveal — skipping the "who could this describe" question is exactly the mistake this activity is built to prevent.

## Common Misconceptions

| Misconception | Why students hold it | Correct it live by |
|---|---|---|
| Anchor the diagram at the person being pointed at/photographed, not the speaker | The photo subject feels like the "main character" of the riddle | Teaching Block B's explicit common-mistakes list, mistake #1 |
| A name in the riddle confirms that person's gender | Everyday assumption carried from real life | Teaching Block B's explicit common-mistakes list, mistake #2 |
| "Only son/daughter of my [parent]" always refers to someone else, never the speaker | Doesn't consider the speaker could be describing themselves | Hook + Activity 1's explicit self-reference resolution |
| You can skip straight to a final answer once the riddle "sounds" simple | Confidence after a few easy examples | Teaching Block B's explicit "skipping links" mistake + Activity 2's forced full reasoning |
| "Son of daughter" and "husband of granddaughter" describe the same kind of relationship | Both involve "grand-" generation words, feels interchangeable | Teaching Block A's explicit direction-matters framing, worked against the Kiran/son-in-law example |
