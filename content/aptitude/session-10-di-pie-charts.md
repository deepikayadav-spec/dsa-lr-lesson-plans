**Duration** 50 min total — **45 min instruction + 5 min buffer** (3 min settling at the start, 2 min flex at the end) · **Topic** Reading pie charts and converting between sector angle, percentage, and value · **Prerequisite** DI Tables and DI Graphs — the percentage-change formula and general DI reading discipline

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, students will be able to:

1. Explain that a pie chart divides 360° proportionally to represent parts of a whole. *(UNDERSTANDING)*
2. Convert between sector angle, percentage, and value using the three standard formulas. *(APPLYING)*
3. Compare two or more sectors, including cases where combined sectors equal another sector exactly. *(ANALYZING)*
4. Apply the four-step interpretation process to a pie-chart-based question. *(APPLYING)*

## Teaching Block A (10–19 min) — TEACH FROM NOTES BELOW

Covers: what a pie chart represents → the three conversion formulas → worked budget example.

**Beats to emphasise**

- **Core idea.** A pie chart divides a circle (360°) into sectors, where each sector's size is proportional to the value it represents. "The larger the slice, the bigger the value" — but the slice alone only tells you a *proportion*; you need the total to get an absolute value.
- **Three formulas, write all on the board together:**
  1. **Angle of a sector = (Value of sector / Total value) × 360°**
  2. **Percentage of a sector = (Value of sector / Total value) × 100%**
  3. **Value of a sector = (Percentage of sector / 100) × Total value**

**The worked data (family monthly budget, total ₹1,20,000):**

| Category | Value (₹) | Percentage | Angle |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rent | 36,000 | 30% | 108° |
| Education | 24,000 | 20% | 72° |
| Food | 18,000 | 15% | 54° |
| Savings | 18,000 | 15% | 54° |
| Transport | 12,000 | 10% | 36° |
| Others | 12,000 | 10% | 36° |
| **Total** | **1,20,000** | **100%** | **360°** |

- **Worked derivation, live — Rent:**
  1. Percentage = (36,000 / 1,20,000) × 100 = **30%**.
  2. Angle = (36,000 / 1,20,000) × 360° = 0.30 × 360 = **108°**.
- **Sanity check, always do this:** percentages across all sectors must sum to 100%, and angles must sum to 360°. Add the table's percentage column live on the board: 30+20+15+15+10+10 = **100 ✔**. Add the angle column: 108+72+54+54+36+36 = **360 ✔**.

**Checkpoint (at 19 min)** — 10 s silent think, cold-call two students:
> *"What's the angle for the Education sector?"*
> **Answer:** (24,000/1,20,000) × 360° = 0.20 × 360 = **72°**.

## ⚡ ALS Activity 1 — Whiteboard Race: Convert the Sector (19–25 min)

**ALS format:** Paired Whiteboard Race — pairs race to convert between angle, percentage, and value for an assigned category, first correct board up wins the round. Chosen to drill the three-formula toolkit into fast recall before Teaching Block B's cross-sector comparison trap.

**Setup line:**
> *"Pairs, boards up, same budget table. I'll call a category and ask for a conversion. First correct board up wins. Three rounds."*

- Round 1: *"Find the angle for Food."* → (18,000/1,20,000) × 360° = **54°**.
- Round 2: *"Find the percentage for Transport."* → (12,000/1,20,000) × 100 = **10%**.
- Round 3: *"If Savings is 15% of a ₹1,20,000 budget, what's its value?"* → (15/100) × 1,20,000 = **₹18,000**.

**How it surfaces:** After each round, ask the winning pair which of the three formulas they used and why — reinforces choosing the right formula for what's given versus what's asked.

**Debrief line:**
> *"Three formulas, but really it's one relationship — value, percentage, and angle are just three ways of describing the same slice. Once you see that, you stop needing to 'memorise' three separate things."*

**Cut rule:** If running short, cut to 2 rounds (drop Round 3), but always require students to name which formula they used.

## Teaching Block B (25–32 min) — TEACH FROM NOTES BELOW

Covers: comparing sectors, including the "different slices, equal totals" trap.

**Beats to emphasise**

- **The trap, posed live:** *"Which is bigger — the Rent slice on its own, or the Food and Savings slices combined?"* Let students guess before revealing (many will assume Rent, the single biggest labelled slice, wins).
- **Worked comparison:**
  - Rent alone = **₹36,000**.
  - Food + Savings combined = 18,000 + 18,000 = **₹36,000**.
  - **They're exactly equal** — despite Rent being drawn as one visually larger slice and Food/Savings being two separate, smaller-looking slices.
- **Say explicitly:** *"This is exactly the kind of question pie-chart problems love to ask — combine two or three smaller sectors and compare them to one bigger-looking one. Never trust the visual size alone; always add the actual values or percentages."*
- **Second worked comparison, live:** *"How much more is spent on Rent than on Transport and Others combined?"* Transport + Others = 12,000+12,000 = 24,000. Rent − (Transport+Others) = 36,000 − 24,000 = **₹12,000 more**.

**Checkpoint (at 32 min)** — cold-call:
> *"Do Education and Transport combined equal, exceed, or fall short of Food alone?"*
> **Answer:** Education+Transport = 24,000+12,000 = 36,000, versus Food = 18,000 → **combined total exceeds Food**, by ₹18,000.

## ⚡ ALS Activity 2 — Silent Solve → Vote-Lock → Reveal: Build Your Own Budget (32–40 min)

**ALS format:** Silent Solve, Vote-Lock, Then Reveal, generative — students design their own 4–5-category pie chart (any total, any proportions) and compute one sector's angle before a few are shared and checked live. Deliberately different register from Activity 1's fast recall race (quiet, individual, constructive), and forces students to apply all three formulas in the direction they choose, not the direction assigned.

**Setup line:**
> *"On your own, three minutes. Design your own budget — pick any total amount and split it into 4 or 5 categories with percentages that add up to 100%. Then calculate the angle for just one of your categories. Write it all down, hold it up when I say show."*

Give 3 minutes of individual construction + calculation, then: *"Show me — three, two, one, show."*

**How it surfaces:** Cold-call 2–3 students to state their total, their categories' percentages, and the one angle they calculated. For each, have the class quickly verify: do the percentages sum to 100%? Does the stated angle match (percentage/100)×360°?

**The reveal / class-check, step by step (using one volunteer's example as a model):**
1. State total and category percentages.
2. Confirm percentages sum to **100%** — if not, the chart itself is invalid before any angle can be trusted.
3. Recompute the claimed angle as (percentage/100) × 360° and confirm it matches what the student calculated.

**Debrief line:**
> *"Building your own chart forces you to hold the whole 100%/360° constraint in your head, not just react to numbers I hand you. That constraint — everything must add up — is your built-in error check on every pie chart question you'll ever see."*

**Cut rule:** If running short, cut construction time to 2 minutes and check only 1–2 volunteers' examples instead of 2–3, but always perform the percentage-sums-to-100 verification live — that check is the actual teaching point.

## Common Misconceptions

| Misconception | Why students hold it | Correct it live by |
|---|---|---|
| A visually bigger single slice always beats two smaller combined slices | Visual size dominates over calculation | Teaching Block B's Rent vs. Food+Savings equal-value reveal |
| A pie chart alone tells you exact values | Slices look like they carry absolute meaning | Poll Q5 + explicit "proportion only, unless total is given" framing |
| The three formulas (angle/percentage/value) are three separate things to memorise | Each was introduced as its own bullet point | Teaching Block A's "one relationship, three views" framing |
| Percentages/angles not summing to 100%/360° is a minor rounding issue | Doesn't treat the sum-check as a real validation step | Activity 2's live percentage-sum verification on student-built charts |
| Comparing sectors just means comparing their visual slice size | No habit of adding values before comparing | Teaching Block B's explicit combined-sector worked examples |
