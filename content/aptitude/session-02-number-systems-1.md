# Number Systems: Basic Classification of Numbers & Divisibility Rules

**Duration** 43 min total — 38 min instruction + 5 min Classroom Quiz · **Topic** Number Systems — classification of numbers and divisibility rules for 2–12, co-primes · **Prerequisite** Introduction to Quantitative Aptitude
**Session type** Lecture · **Format** Condensed — active learning strategies referenced by name, single closing quiz

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, students will be able to:

1. Classify a given number as real or imaginary, and — within the reals — as rational or irrational, integer, whole, natural, or fraction. *(REMEMBERING)*
2. Explain why a number is irrational using the p/q test, distinguishing it from a decimal that merely looks long or awkward. *(UNDERSTANDING)*
3. Apply the divisibility rules for 2 through 12 to test whether a given number is divisible by each, including the multi-step rule of 7. *(APPLYING)*
4. Use co-primes and successive-division reasoning to solve chained remainder/quotient word problems. *(ANALYZING)*

## Concept Walkthrough (9–24 min)

**Core idea:** Numbers split into Real / Imaginary. Real splits into Rational / Irrational — rational means expressible as p/q (terminates or repeats as a decimal), irrational means neither. Rational splits further into Integers and Fractions. Divisibility rules for 2–12 are shortcuts, not new facts each time — 6 = divisible by 2 **and** 3, 12 = divisible by 3 **and** 4, no new rule needed.

**Worked example — rule of 7 on 343:** unit digit = `3`. `3 × 2 = 6`. Remaining digits = `34`. `34 − 6 = 28`. `28` is divisible by 7 → **343 is divisible by 7.** This is the one divisibility rule that's a genuine multi-step algorithm, not a glance-and-check.

**Checkpoint:**
> *"Using the combo rule, is 3546 divisible by 6?"*
> **Answer:** Yes — 3546 is even (rule of 2), and its digit sum 3+5+4+6=18 is divisible by 3 (rule of 3), so it passes both and is divisible by 6.

## ⚡ Active Learning Strategy 1 — Think-Pair-Share: Rational or Irrational? (24–31 min)

Six numbers (including `22/7`, `π`, and a repeating decimal) sorted into two buckets, individually first, then in pairs. Exposes two classic traps at once: mistaking an approximation of π for π itself, and mistaking "never seems to end" for "irrational" when it's actually a repeating decimal.

---

## ⚡ Active Learning Strategy 2 — Full-Chain Dictation: Co-Primes & Remainder Word Problems (31–38 min)

Instructor writes *only* what a student dictates, one step at a time, working through a chained-remainder word problem (divide by 7, then 11, then 12) with nothing filled in or assumed. Exposes whether students can reconstruct a multi-step chain themselves rather than just following a worked slide.

## Common Misconceptions

| Misconception | Why students hold it | Correct it live by |
|---|---|---|
| `22/7` and `3.14` are irrational because they approximate π | Familiarity with π's irrationality bleeds onto anything used to estimate it | ALS 1 — writing both as exact p/q fractions live |
| A long, never-ending decimal is automatically irrational | "It never ends" gets mistaken for "it has no pattern" | ALS 1 — running the p/q conversion on a repeating decimal right next to a genuinely non-repeating one |
| Rule of 11: subtracting the wrong digit-set from the other | Easy to flip which set (odd- or even-placed digits) goes first | Labelling odd- and even-placed digits explicitly before subtracting, every time |
| "Co-prime" means "prime" | The words look related | Giving a co-prime pair where neither number is prime (e.g. 8 and 9) |
| Word problems like the chained-remainder one need a clean algebraic formula | School conditioning to always "solve for x" | ALS 2 — modelling the assume-and-verify approach as a legitimate, fast technique |
