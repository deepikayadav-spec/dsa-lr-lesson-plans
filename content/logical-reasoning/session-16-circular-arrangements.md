# Session 16 — Data Arrangements: Circular Arrangements

**Duration** 50 min total — **45 min instruction + 5 min buffer** (3 min settling at the start, 2 min flex at the end) · **Topic** Facing-direction logic (inward/outward), opposite/adjacent positions, and multi-clue circular puzzles · **Prerequisite** Linear Arrangements — terminology and multi-clue solving discipline, applied here with a rotating reference frame
**Session type** Lecture + guided practice. No source slide deck — content from the session's concept-explanation text. ---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, students will be able to:

1. Explain why circular arrangements have no fixed start/end, unlike linear rows. *(UNDERSTANDING)*
2. Apply the facing-direction rule (inward: left=anticlockwise, right=clockwise; outward: left=clockwise, right=anticlockwise) correctly. *(APPLYING)*
3. Find the position opposite a given person in an even-numbered circle. *(APPLYING)*
4. Solve a multi-clue circular arrangement by fixing one position first, then applying directional clues systematically. *(APPLYING)*

`A` Somewhere on the left half · `B` The very first position, no exceptions
→ *Read:* B is correct.

**Q2.** What's the correct order to solve a multi-clue arrangement?
`A` Top to bottom, in the order clues are given · `B` Most certain clues (extremes, immediates) first, then work outward
→ *Read:* B is correct.

**Q3.** "E is between D and F" — how many possible arrangements does that alone allow?
`A` 1 · `B` 2 · `C` 3
→ *Read:* B is correct (D_E_F or F_E_D).

## Hook (7–10 min)

Say: *"You and six friends are sitting around a circular table. Your friend Arya says, 'I am sitting to the immediate right of Rahul.' Simple enough, right? Now — does that tell you exactly where Arya is sitting?"*

Take guesses. Most will say yes.

> *"It doesn't — not until you know one more thing: is Rahul facing into the table, or away from it? In a row, left and right never change. In a circle, they flip completely depending on which way someone's facing. That's the entire topic today, and it's the single most common trap in circular arrangement questions."*

---

## Teaching Block A (10–19 min) — TEACH FROM NOTES BELOW

Covers: why circles are different from rows → the facing-direction framework → opposite positions.

**Beats to emphasise**

- **No fixed start or end.** A row has a definite first and last position. A circle is a loop — there's no "position 1" until you choose one arbitrarily as a reference point. *"Clues are relative, not absolute, because the meaning of left and right itself depends on which way each person faces."*
- **The facing-direction framework — write this exactly on the board:**
  - **Facing Inward** (toward the center): **Left = Anticlockwise, Right = Clockwise.**
  - **Facing Outward** (away from the center): **Left = Clockwise, Right = Anticlockwise.**
  - *"Notice they're exact opposites of each other — that's the whole trap. Always confirm which way people are facing before applying either rule."*
- **Opposite positions.** In an even-numbered circle (6, 8, 10 people), the person "opposite" someone is exactly halfway around — for 6 people, that's 3 seats away in either direction.
- **Worked example, live, 6 people facing inward, clockwise positions 1–6:** *"If P is at position 1, who's directly opposite P?"* Halfway around a 6-seat circle = 3 seats → **Position 4.**

**Checkpoint (at 19 min)** — 10 s silent think, cold-call two students:
> *"Six people sit facing inward. If someone is at position 3, and 'right' means clockwise for someone facing inward, who's immediately to their right?"*
> **Answer:** **Position 4** (clockwise neighbour of position 3).

## ⚡ ALS Activity 1 — Human Circle (19–25 min)

**ALS format:** Physical Demo / Kinesthetic Modeling — 6 student volunteers form a circle, first facing inward, then facing outward, physically testing "immediate right" and "immediate left" under both framings. Chosen because the facing-direction rule is the single hardest idea in this topic, and having students physically turn around and re-test "right" makes the flip impossible to forget.

**Setup line:**
> *"Six volunteers, form a circle, everyone face the center — that's facing inward. When I say 'take a step to your right,' actually do it, and everyone watch which way the circle rotates."*

- **Round 1 (facing inward):** *"Everyone step to your right."* — the whole circle should visibly rotate **clockwise** (as seen from above/by the class). Confirm: *"Right, facing inward, is clockwise — exactly what's on the board."*
- **Round 2:** *"Now everyone turn around, face outward, away from the center. Step to your right again."* — this time the circle rotates **anticlockwise**. Confirm: *"Same word, 'right' — completely opposite physical movement, just because you turned around."*
- **Round 3 (apply a clue live):** *"Facing inward again. [Name], you're my fixed point. [Name2], go stand immediately to their right."* Confirm as a class the correct clockwise neighbour was chosen.

**How it surfaces:** After Round 2, ask: *"Who's confused right now?"* — normalise the confusion, then immediately re-run Round 1 side by side with Round 2 so the contrast is visible back to back.

**Debrief line:**
> *"You just felt, physically, why circular arrangement questions always tell you which way people are facing before giving directional clues — because without that one fact, 'right' is meaningless."*

**Cut rule:** If space is tight, do this seated with hand gestures (point right) instead of full-body stepping, but always run both the inward and outward rounds back to back — the contrast is the entire point.

## Teaching Block B (25–32 min) — TEACH FROM NOTES BELOW

Covers: the problem-solving strategy, applied to a full 6-person circular puzzle.

**Beats to emphasise**

- **Problem-solving strategy, write on the board:**
  1. **Read all clues once** before drawing anything.
  2. **Fix one person's position first** — pick a concrete clue (often "opposite" or a stated fixed seat) to anchor the whole diagram.
  3. **Apply directional clues systematically**, always double-checking facing direction first.
  4. **Use elimination** for whoever's left once the direct clues are exhausted.
- **Worked example, live, 6 people (P, Q, R, S, T, U), all facing inward:**
  1. P sits opposite U.
  2. Q sits immediately to P's right.
  3. R sits immediately to P's left.
  4. S sits opposite Q.
  5. T is adjacent to U.
  - **Solve, step by step:** Anchor P at position 1 (clue-free choice, since circles have no fixed start). Facing inward, right=clockwise → Q = position 2 (clue 2). Left=anticlockwise → R = position 6 (clue 3). Opposite P(1) in a 6-circle = position 4 → U = position 4 (clue 1). Opposite Q(2) = position 5 → S = position 5 (clue 4). Only position 3 remains → T = position 3 (clue 5, confirmed adjacent to U at position 4). **Final: P(1), Q(2), T(3), U(4), S(5), R(6).**
- **Say explicitly:** *"We picked P's seat arbitrarily — in a circle, only the *relative* positions matter, not which physical seat is 'number 1.' That's different from linear arrangements, where extreme-left genuinely means something fixed."*

**Checkpoint (at 32 min)** — cold-call:
> *"In the arrangement above, who is immediately to U's left, facing inward?"*
> **Answer:** Anticlockwise neighbour of U(4) = position 3 = **T**.

## ⚡ ALS Activity 2 — Silent Solve → Vote-Lock → Reveal: Flip to Outward (32–40 min)

**ALS format:** Silent Solve, Vote-Lock, Then Reveal — students solve a fresh circular puzzle where everyone faces OUTWARD instead of inward, deliberately testing whether the flipped rule from Activity 1 actually stuck. Different register from Activity 1's loud physical demo (quiet, individual, single big reveal).

**Setup line:**
> *"On your own, three minutes. Six people, A through F, sit facing OUTWARD this time — away from the center. Clues:*
> *1. A is fixed at a seat. 2. B sits immediately to A's right. 3. C sits immediately to A's left. 4. D sits opposite A. 5. F sits immediately to D's left.*
> *Find the full arrangement. Write it out, hold it up when I say show."*

Give 3 minutes of silent solo work, then: *"Show me — three, two, one, show."*

**The reveal, step by step:**
1. Anchor A at position 1.
2. **Facing outward: Right = Anticlockwise.** B = anticlockwise neighbour of A(1) = **position 6**.
3. **Facing outward: Left = Clockwise.** C = clockwise neighbour of A(1) = **position 2**.
4. D opposite A(1) in a 6-circle = **position 4**.
5. F immediately to D's left (outward, clockwise) = **position 5**.
6. Only position 3 remains, unassigned → **E = position 3**, found purely by elimination.
7. **Final: A(1), C(2), E(3), D(4), F(5), B(6).**

**Debrief line:**
> *"If you used the inward rule out of habit here, your B and C would be swapped from the correct answer. That's exactly the mistake this whole session was built to prevent — always check facing direction first, every single time, never assume."*

**Cut rule:** If running short, cut the silent window to 2 minutes but always show the inward-vs-outward contrast explicitly in the reveal (state where B and C *would* have gone under the wrong rule) — that contrast is the actual teaching point.

## Common Misconceptions

| Misconception | Why students hold it | Correct it live by |
|---|---|---|
| "Right" means the same physical direction regardless of facing | Row-based intuition carried over from Linear Arrangements | Poll Q4 + Activity 1's Human Circle, both rounds back to back |
| Circular arrangements have a "real" starting seat | Rows always have a definite position 1 | Teaching Block B's explicit "anchor arbitrarily" framing |
| "Opposite" only applies in circles with an obvious even split | Doesn't generalise the "halfway around" rule | Teaching Block A's explicit worked opposite-position example |
| Facing inward and outward use the same left/right rule, just mirrored slightly | Underestimates that the rules are complete opposites | Activity 1's explicit side-by-side inward/outward contrast |
| Once you know the facing direction, you don't need to re-check it for every clue | Assumes it only needs to be established once, loosely | Activity 2's explicit "always check first, every time" debrief |
