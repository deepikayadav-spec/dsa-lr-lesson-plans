## Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, students will be able to:

1. Convert unstructured paragraph clues into a structured grid, tracking confirmations and eliminations. *(APPLYING)*
2. Solve a multi-entity paragraph puzzle (people/pets/locations, etc.) by cross-referencing clues systematically. *(ANALYZING)*
3. Identify a consistent operation or pattern across the rows of a matrix-style puzzle. *(ANALYZING)*
4. Avoid common pitfalls — assuming symmetry, overlooking simple operations, concluding before testing every clue. *(EVALUATING)*

## Warm-Up Poll — Diagnostic (3–7 min) · ALS: Polling

> New topic — Puzzles doesn't build directly on Cubes, so this poll is diagnostic, not retrieval, though it draws on several earlier topics.

Say: *"Five quick questions before we start."*

**Q1.** When a puzzle gives you a paragraph of clues about several people, what should you do first?
`A` Try to solve it in your head · `B` List out all the entities (names, categories) before reading clues again · `C` Guess an answer and check it
→ *Read:* B is correct.

**Q2.** Which earlier topics used a similar "clues → grid → solve" approach?
`A` Ranking and Directions only · `B` Linear/Circular Arrangements and Blood Relations · `C` Both of the above
→ *Read:* C is correct — puzzles combine skills from several earlier sessions.

**Q3.** Have you solved a "who owns which pet" or similar multi-clue logic puzzle before?
`A` Never · `B` Once or twice · `C` Regularly
→ *Read:* If mostly A, spend extra time on the grid method before speeding up.

**Q4.** Quick riddle: three friends, three pets, three house colors. Clue 1: the teacher lives in the red house. Clue 2: the doctor owns a cat. Clue 3: the person with the bird lives in the blue house. Can you guess who owns the dog?
`A` The teacher · `B` The doctor · `C` Not enough information · `D` Not sure
→ *Read:* A is correct — and it IS solvable with just these three clues, which surprises most people. Don't confirm yet, this is the Hook, solved fully in Teaching Block A.

**Q5.** How comfortable are you spotting a pattern across a grid of numbers (not just a straight line/series)?
`A` Very uncomfortable · `B` Okay with practice · `C` Comfortable
→ *Read:* If mostly A, slow down through Teaching Block B's matrix examples.

**Running it** — poll tool, ~45 s per question, ~3.75 min total.

## Hook (7–10 min)

Say: *"Three friends, three pets, three house colors. The teacher lives in the red house. The doctor owns a cat. The person with the bird lives in the blue house. Can you figure out who owns the dog?"*

Give 30 seconds, take guesses, then say: *"Hold that thought — we're about to solve this properly, with a method that works even when a puzzle has ten clues instead of three."*

## Teaching Block A (10–19 min) — TEACH FROM NOTES BELOW

<!-- placement: inferred grouping — no source deck; grouped from GitBook Concept Explanation extraction -->
Covers: the paragraph-puzzle method, solving the Hook fully, live.

**Beats to emphasise**

- **Paragraph-puzzle method, write on the board:**
  1. **List the entities first** — every person, pet, colour, or category mentioned, before touching the clues again.
  2. **Extract relational clues carefully** — watch for phrases like "next to," "owns," "on the right of."
  3. **Build a grid** with rows and columns for each category, using checkmarks (✔) for confirmed matches and crosses (✘) for eliminated ones.
- **Solve the Hook, live, step by step:**
  1. Entities: People {Teacher, Doctor, Person X}. Pets {Cat, Bird, Dog}. Colours {Red, Blue, Colour Y}.
  2. Clue 1: Teacher → Red house. Mark it.
  3. Clue 2: Doctor → Cat. Mark it.
  4. Clue 3: Bird owner → Blue house. Since Teacher is already Red (not Blue), the Teacher can't be the bird owner — so the bird owner must be Doctor or Person X. But Doctor already owns the Cat (clue 2), so Doctor can't also own the Bird → **Person X owns the Bird, and lives in the Blue house.**
  5. That leaves the Dog for whoever's left — Doctor already has Cat, Person X already has Bird → **the Teacher owns the Dog.**
- **Say explicitly:** *"Notice we never directly stated 'Teacher owns Dog' — we eliminated everything else until only one option remained. That's the entire engine behind paragraph puzzles: confirm what you can, eliminate what you can't, and let the last option fall out on its own."*

**Checkpoint (at 19 min)** — 10 s silent think, cold-call two students:
> *"In the solved puzzle above, what colour house does the Doctor live in?"*
> **Answer:** Colours used so far: Teacher=Red, Person X=Blue → **the Doctor gets the remaining third colour** (unnamed in the original clues, but logically the only one left).

## ⚡ ALS Activity 1 — Whiteboard Race: Mini Paragraph Puzzles (19–25 min)

**ALS format:** Paired Whiteboard Race — pairs race to solve a small 3-clue, 3-entity paragraph puzzle using the grid method, first correct board up wins the round. Chosen to drill the "list, extract, eliminate" discipline into fast recall before Teaching Block B shifts to matrix-pattern puzzles.

**Setup line:**
> *"Pairs, boards up. I'll give you a mini paragraph puzzle — sketch your grid, work by elimination. First correct board up wins. Two rounds (these take longer than a single calculation)."*

- Round 1: *"Three students: Aisha, Ben, Cara. Three subjects: Math, Art, Music. Aisha doesn't study Art. Ben studies Music. Who studies Math?"* → Ben=Music. Aisha≠Art, and Aisha≠Music(Ben has it) → **Aisha=Math.** Cara gets the remaining subject, Art.
- Round 2: *"Three runners: X, Y, Z finish 1st, 2nd, 3rd. X doesn't finish 1st. Z finishes right after X. Who finishes 1st?"* → X≠1st. "Z right after X" means Z's position = X's position+1, so X can't be 3rd either (no position after 3rd) → X=2nd, Z=3rd → **Y finishes 1st** (only position left).

**How it surfaces:** After each round, have the winning pair state which clue they used first and why — reinforces starting with the most restrictive clue, not just reading top to bottom.

**Debrief line:**
> *"Both puzzles solved by elimination, not by being told the answer directly. That's the whole skill — and it scales up to much bigger puzzles the exact same way."*

**Cut rule:** If running short, keep only Round 1 — these take longer per round than typical whiteboard races, so one solid round beats two rushed ones.

## Teaching Block B (25–32 min) — TEACH FROM NOTES BELOW

Covers: matrix/pattern-based puzzles (a text-representable equivalent of the source's figure-based puzzles), and common pitfalls.

**Beats to emphasise**

- **Matrix puzzles — the same pattern-spotting skill, applied to a grid instead of a straight series.** *"Observe operations across rows (or diagonals), and check for consistency across every row before deciding on a pattern."*
- **Worked example, live (instructor-authored — a text-based equivalent of a figure-pattern puzzle):**

| Row | Col 1 | Col 2 | Col 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3 | 6 | 9 |
| 2 | 5 | 10 | 15 |
| 3 | 7 | 14 | ? |

  1. Check Row 1: 3, 6, 9 — that's ×2, then ×1.5? No — check differently: 3, 3×2=6, 3×3=9. **Pattern: n, 2n, 3n.**
  2. Verify against Row 2: 5, 5×2=10, 5×3=15. **Matches** — same n, 2n, 3n pattern.
  3. Apply to Row 3: n=7 → 7, 14, 7×3=**21**.
- **Common pitfalls, write on the board:**
  1. **Assuming symmetry incorrectly** — don't assume a pattern must be "balanced" or mirror itself without checking.
  2. **Overlooking simple operations** — squaring, factorials, or simple multiplication are easy to miss if you're only checking addition/subtraction.
  3. **Concluding without testing every row** — a pattern that fits Row 1 must be verified against Row 2 (and Row 3, if given) before you trust it, exactly like Teaching Block A's elimination discipline.

**Checkpoint (at 32 min)** — cold-call:
> *"In the matrix above, what would Row 4 look like if it started with 10?"*
> **Answer:** n=10 → **10, 20, 30**.

## ⚡ ALS Activity 2 — Silent Solve → Vote-Lock → Reveal: Find the Missing Cell (32–40 min)

**ALS format:** Silent Solve, Vote-Lock, Then Reveal — students find a matrix's pattern and the missing value entirely on their own before the reveal. Deliberately different register from Activity 1's loud paired race (quiet, individual, single big reveal).

**Setup line:**
> *"On your own, two minutes. Find the pattern and the missing value:*
> *Row 1: 4, 8, 16*
> *Row 2: 5, 10, 20*
> *Row 3: 6, 12, ?*
> *Write your answer and the pattern rule, hold it up when I say show."*

Give 2 minutes silent work, then: *"Show me — three, two, one, show."*

**The reveal, step by step:**
1. Check Row 1: 4, 4×2=8, 4×4=16. **Pattern: n, 2n, 4n.**
2. Verify against Row 2: 5, 5×2=10, 5×4=20. **Matches.**
3. Apply to Row 3: n=6 → 6, 12, 6×4=**24**.

**Debrief line:**
> *"Notice this pattern (n, 2n, 4n) looks almost identical to Teaching Block B's (n, 2n, 3n) at first glance — the middle column is the same shape in both. If you only checked the first two columns before deciding, you'd have gotten this wrong. Always verify against every column, every row, before locking in an answer."*

**Cut rule:** If running short, cut the silent window to 90 seconds but always show the explicit "this looks like Teaching Block B's pattern but isn't" comparison in the reveal — that comparison is the actual lesson.

## Exit Ticket + Homework (45–48 min)

**Exit ticket** (~1 min):

> Three siblings: A, B, C, ages 10, 12, 14 in some order. A is not the youngest. C is older than A. Who is 14?
> **Answer:** A≠youngest, so A is middle(12) or oldest(14). C>A, so C can't be younger than A, meaning A isn't the oldest (14) — because then C would need to be older than 14, impossible → A=12 (middle). C>A=12, so C=14. **C is 14.** (B gets the remaining age, 10.)

Scan responses on the way out — if the elimination discipline from Teaching Block A isn't sticking, revisit briefly before Session 24.

**Homework**

| Task | Note |
|---|---|
| Four friends, four fruits, three clues given — construct your own 4-entity paragraph puzzle and solve it the next day | Self-check — construction reinforces the list/extract/eliminate method |
| Row 1: 2, 4, 8. Row 2: 3, 6, 12. Row 3: 5, 10, ? — find the pattern and missing value | Self-check using the matrix method from Teaching Block B |

Tell them: *"Puzzles pulled together almost everything from this course — elimination, grids, and patterns. Session 24 is the last one — Data Sufficiency, where you judge whether you have ENOUGH information, without necessarily solving anything at all."*

## Common Misconceptions

| Misconception | Why students hold it | Correct it live by |
|---|---|---|
| A puzzle needs many clues to be solvable — 3 clues "can't be enough" | Underestimates how much elimination logic can extract from few clues | Hook + Teaching Block A's fully-solved 3-clue puzzle |
| A pattern that fits the first row/column is safe to trust immediately | Skips verifying against the remaining rows | Teaching Block B's explicit "verify every row" discipline + Activity 2's lookalike-pattern trap |
| Matrix puzzles need a completely different method from paragraph puzzles | Different presentation (grid of numbers vs. sentences) feels unrelated | Explicit "same pattern-spotting skill" framing linking Teaching Blocks A and B |
| The most "obvious" pattern (e.g. simple addition) is always the right one | Confirmation bias toward the first pattern noticed | Teaching Block B's explicit pitfall #2 (overlooking simple operations like multiplication) |
| Elimination-based answers are "guesses" rather than proven | Doesn't recognise elimination as valid logical proof | Teaching Block A's explicit "confirm, eliminate, last option falls out" framing |
