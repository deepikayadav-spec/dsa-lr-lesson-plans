# Session 13 — Coding & Decoding: Odd Man Out & Analogy

**Duration** 50 min total — **45 min instruction + 5 min buffer** (3 min settling at the start, 2 min flex at the end) · **Topic** Finding the outlier in a group (numbers/letters), and solving word/number/letter analogies · **Prerequisite** Number Series and Alphabet Series — pattern-checking discipline, applied here to spot outliers and relationships
**Session type** Lecture + guided practice. No source slide deck — content from the session's concept-explanation text. No classroom quiz bank yet — 5-min slot reserved at end. the "Problem Solving" page is image-only and unrecoverable — all practice problems below are instructor-authored. · **Format** 50-min recalibrated, 2 ALS activities

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, students will be able to:

1. Identify the odd one out in a group of numbers or letters by testing arithmetic, geometric, number-property, or alternating patterns. *(ANALYZING)*
2. Explain analogy as a transferable relationship between two pairs, and classify common analogy types. *(UNDERSTANDING)*
3. Solve analogy questions by identifying the relationship in the given pair and applying it to find the missing term. *(APPLYING)*
4. Use options-elimination as a strategy when the relationship isn't immediately obvious. *(EVALUATING)*

## Hook (7–10 min)

Say: *"2, 4, 6, 9 — which one doesn't belong, and more importantly, why?"* Take a couple of answers, then confirm: *"9 — because 2, 4, 6 are all even, and 9 breaks that. Simple once you see it."*

> *"Now the harder version: Tree is to Leaf, as Book is to...?"* Take guesses. Confirm: *"Page — a tree's smallest visible part is a leaf, a book's smallest visible part is a page."*

> *"Two skills, one session: spotting what breaks a pattern, and spotting what connects two things. Both come up constantly in exams, and both come down to the same habit — naming the relationship explicitly, out loud, before you answer."*

## Teaching Block A (10–19 min) — TEACH FROM NOTES BELOW

Covers: Odd Man Out — logic types and worked examples across numbers and letters.

**Beats to emphasise**

- **Four logic types to check, in order (same discipline as Number Series):**
  1. **Arithmetic patterns** — fixed addition/subtraction.
  2. **Geometric patterns** — multiplication/division.
  3. **Number properties** — primes, perfect squares, perfect cubes, odd/even.
  4. **Alternating patterns** — a repeating sequence with a variation.
- **Worked example, live (from the source, verified):** *"2, 4, 6, 8, 11 — which is the odd one out?"*
  1. Check arithmetic: differences should be constant +2 (2→4→6→8, all +2 so far).
  2. 8→11 is +3, not +2 — breaks the pattern.
  3. **11 is the odd one out.**
- **For alphabet groups, convert to numbers first, then apply the same checks.** *"B, D, F, I"* → positions 2, 4, 6, 9 → same pattern as the number example above → **I is the odd one out** (should be H, position 8, to keep +2 throughout).
- **Say explicitly:** *"Always name which of the four logic types you're testing, out loud or on paper. 'It just looks different' is not an answer an exam will accept — 'the difference breaks from +2 to +3' is."*

**Checkpoint (at 19 min)** — 10 s silent think, cold-call two students:
> *"3, 9, 27, 80, 243 — which is the odd one out, and why?"*
> **Answer:** Geometric pattern, ×3 each time (3→9→27→81→243) → **80 is the odd one out** (should be 81).

## ⚡ ALS Activity 1 — Whiteboard Race: Spot the Outlier (19–25 min)

**ALS format:** Paired Whiteboard Race — pairs race to find the outlier in an assigned group (numbers or letters) and name the broken logic type, first correct board up wins the round. Chosen to build fast, disciplined outlier-spotting before Teaching Block B introduces analogy.

**Setup line:**
> *"Pairs, boards up. I'll give you a group of numbers or letters — find the outlier AND name which logic type it breaks. First correct board up wins. Three rounds."*

- Round 1: *"5, 10, 15, 22, 25"* → arithmetic +5 → **22 is the outlier** (should be 20).
- Round 2: *"C, E, G, J, K"* → positions 3,5,7,9,11(+2 arithmetic) → K=11 fits actually check: 3,5,7,9,11 all +2, so recompute — use *"C, F, I, L, N"* instead → positions 3,6,9,12,14 → should be +3 each → **N is the outlier** (should be O, position 15).
- Round 3: *"4, 9, 16, 20, 36"* → perfect squares (2²,3²,4²,5²,6²=4,9,16,25,36) → **20 is the outlier** (should be 25).

**How it surfaces:** After each round, have the winning pair state the broken logic type out loud before revealing the outlier.

**Debrief line:**
> *"Notice you used three different logic types across three rounds — arithmetic, arithmetic again with letters, and number properties. The method is identical every time: name the pattern, find where it breaks."*

**Cut rule:** If running short, cut to 2 rounds (drop Round 3), but always require the logic type to be named.

## Teaching Block B (25–32 min) — TEACH FROM NOTES BELOW

Covers: Analogy — relationship types, worked examples, and the elimination strategy.

**Beats to emphasise**

- **Core idea.** Analogy is "simply a comparison" — a relationship established between one pair of items applies the same way to a second pair.
- **Common analogy categories, write on the board:**
  - **Part-whole:** Tree : Leaf :: Book : Page.
  - **Mathematical operation:** 3 : 9 :: 5 : 25 (relationship: square the first number).
  - **Position-based letter shift:** A : C :: D : F (relationship: +2 positions).
  - **Word relationships:** synonyms, opposites, categories (e.g. Hot : Cold :: Up : Down, an opposites relationship).
- **Worked example, live:** *"Doctor : Hospital :: Teacher : ?"* — relationship is "person : their primary workplace" → **School**.
- **Worked example, live:** *"4 : 16 :: 6 : ?"* — relationship is "square the first number" (4²=16) → 6² = **36**.
- **Elimination strategy:** *"If the relationship isn't obvious from the first pair alone, look at the answer options — they often reveal what kind of relationship (mathematical, positional, categorical) is expected, narrowing your search."*

**Checkpoint (at 32 min)** — cold-call:
> *"B : D :: F : ?, and what's the relationship?"*
> **Answer:** Relationship is +2 positions (B→D is +2) → F(+2) = **H**.

## ⚡ ALS Activity 2 — Silent Solve → Vote-Lock → Reveal: Name Both (32–40 min)

**ALS format:** Silent Solve, Vote-Lock, Then Reveal — students solve one Odd Man Out and one Analogy question together, committing to both the answer AND the named logic/relationship before the reveal. Deliberately different register from Activity 1's loud paired race (quiet, individual, single big reveal), and explicitly closes both halves of the session opened in the Hook.

**Setup line:**
> *"On your own, two minutes, two questions. First: 6, 12, 18, 25, 30 — find the outlier and name the logic. Second: Fish : Water :: Bird : ? — find the answer and name the relationship. Write both, hold up when I say show."*

Give 2 minutes silent work, then: *"Show me — three, two, one, show."*

**The reveal, step by step:**
1. **Odd Man Out:** arithmetic pattern, +6 each time (6,12,18,24,30) → **25 is the outlier** (should be 24).
2. **Analogy:** relationship is "creature : its natural habitat" (Fish lives in Water) → Bird lives in → **Sky / Air**.

**Debrief line:**
> *"Both questions today came down to the exact same habit — name the relationship or the pattern explicitly, out loud, before you commit to an answer. That single habit is the whole topic."*

**Cut rule:** If running short, keep only the Odd Man Out question (drop the Analogy question) but always require the logic type to be named in the reveal.

## Common Misconceptions

| Misconception | Why students hold it | Correct it live by |
|---|---|---|
| "It just looks different" is a valid enough reason to call something the outlier | No habit of explicitly naming the broken pattern | Teaching Block A's explicit "name the logic type" requirement |
| Analogy relationships are always about meaning, never math | First examples encountered are usually word-pairs | Teaching Block B's explicit mathematical-operation and position-shift categories |
| If the first pair's relationship isn't obvious, the question is unsolvable | Doesn't know the options-elimination fallback strategy | Teaching Block B's explicit elimination-strategy tip |
| Odd Man Out and Analogy are unrelated skills bundled together for convenience | They look like separate question formats | Hook's explicit "one habit, two question types" framing |
| Alphabet-based odd-man-out groups need a different method than number groups | Letters "feel" different from numbers | Teaching Block A's explicit "convert to numbers first" framing |
