## Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, students will be able to:

1. Apply the Tick (✔) and Cross (✘) shortcut to each of the four statement types without drawing a full Venn diagram. *(APPLYING)*
2. State the four rules for combining two statements into a valid conclusion. *(REMEMBERING)*
3. Combine two Tick-and-Cross statements to derive a valid conclusion, or correctly determine that none exists. *(APPLYING)*
4. Recognise the one situation where the Tick and Cross method needs extra care — reversed/direction-sensitive statements. *(EVALUATING)*

## Warm-Up Poll — Retrieval Practice on Session 20 (3–7 min) · ALS: Polling

Say: *"Five quick ones from last session."*

**Q1.** What are the two conditions required for "Either I or II follows" to be correct?
`A` Any two opposite-sounding conclusions · `B` Neither follows alone, AND they form a genuine complementary pair
→ *Read:* B is correct.

**Q2.** "All squares are rectangles" — can this be reversed to "All rectangles are squares"?
`A` Yes · `B` No
→ *Read:* B is correct.

**Q3.** "Some A are B" — does this always mean "Some B are A" too?
`A` Yes · `B` No
→ *Read:* A is correct — this symmetry will matter again today.

**Q4.** Quick riddle: "All apples are fruits." Can I confidently say "Some fruits are apples"?
`A` Yes, safely · `B` It happens to be true here, but the wording needs care · `C` No, never true
→ *Read:* B is the nuanced correct framing — most will guess A too quickly. Don't over-explain yet, seeds today's Hook.

**Q5.** How comfortable are you with drawing a full Venn diagram for every single syllogism question, given exam time pressure?
`A` It takes too long · `B` It's fine, I'm fast at it · `C` Not sure
→ *Read:* If mostly A, today's shortcut is exactly the fix.

**Running it** — poll tool, ~45 s per question, ~3.75 min total.

## Hook (7–10 min)

Say: *"Quick puzzle: if I say 'All apples are fruits,' can I confidently say 'Some fruits are apples'? Most people say yes instantly — but that's exactly where logic tricks us."*

> *"It happens to be true in this case — but the reasoning that gets you there safely isn't 'reverse the sentence,' it's a specific rule about direction. Drawing a full Venn diagram every time you need to check something like this works, but it's slow. Today you get a shortcut — two symbols, a tick and a cross, that do the same job in seconds."*

## Teaching Block A (10–19 min) — TEACH FROM NOTES BELOW

<!-- placement: inferred grouping — no source deck; grouped from GitBook Concept Explanation extraction -->
Covers: the four statement types → the Tick and Cross table, worked live.

**Beats to emphasise**

- **Four standard statement types, same as Session 20, renamed formally:**
  - **Universal Affirmative:** All A's are B's.
  - **Particular Affirmative:** Some A's are B's.
  - **Universal Negative:** No A's are B's.
  - **Particular Negative:** Some A's are not B's.
- **The Tick and Cross table — write it exactly like this on the board:**

| Statement Type | A | B | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| All A's are B's | ✔ | ✘ | A fully inside B |
| No A's are B's | ✔ | ✔ | A and B completely separate |
| Some A's are not B's | ✘ | ✔ | Part of A outside B |
| Some A's are B's | ✘ | ✘ | Partial overlap |

- **Key warning, say explicitly:** *"'Some apples are fruits' doesn't automatically mean 'Some fruits are apples' just because the words got swapped — the direction matters. Always check using the tick, not the wording."*
- **Worked example, live (from the source, verified):** *"All books are papers"* → Books gets **✔**, Papers gets **✘**. *"Now test a conclusion: 'Some papers are books.' Does the reverse hold?"* Since Books is fully inside Papers (✔✘), some Papers ARE Books — reversing "All A are B" to "Some B are A" IS always valid (this is the one safe reversal), because if A is entirely inside B, then B necessarily contains at least all of A.

**Checkpoint (at 19 min)** — 10 s silent think, cold-call two students:
> *"'No pens are erasers' — what tick/cross does Pens get, and what does Erasers get?"*
> **Answer:** Both get **✔** — "No A's are B's" means both terms get a tick (completely separate).

## ⚡ ALS Activity 1 — Whiteboard Race: Tick or Cross? (19–25 min)

**ALS format:** Paired Whiteboard Race — pairs race to assign the correct tick/cross pair for a given statement, first correct board up wins the round. Chosen to drill the table into instant recall before Teaching Block B introduces combining two statements.

**Setup line:**
> *"Pairs, boards up, table visible. I'll give you a statement — write the tick/cross for both terms. First correct board up wins. Three rounds."*

- Round 1: *"Some dogs are pets."* → Dogs=**✘**, Pets=**✘**.
- Round 2: *"All teachers are educated."* → Teachers=**✔**, Educated=**✘**.
- Round 3: *"Some birds are not flightless."* → Birds=**✘**, Flightless=**✔**.

**How it surfaces:** After each round, ask the winning pair to state which statement type it was (Universal Affirmative, etc.) before revealing the ticks — reinforces the vocabulary from Teaching Block A alongside the shortcut itself.

**Debrief line:**
> *"Four statement types, four fixed tick/cross patterns — memorise the table once, and you never need to sketch a circle again for a single statement."*

**Cut rule:** If running short, cut to 2 rounds (drop Round 3), but always require the statement type to be named.

## Teaching Block B (25–32 min) — TEACH FROM NOTES BELOW

Covers: the four rules for combining two statements, worked live.

**Beats to emphasise**

- **Four rules, write on the board:**
  1. **Common term needs at least one tick.** If the term shared by both statements has NO tick anywhere (all crosses), no conclusion is possible.
  2. **Two statements need three terms total, with exactly one common term** linking them.
  3. **Both statements particular (both have a ✘✘ pattern) → no conclusion.**
  4. **Both statements negative (both have a ✔✔ pattern) → no conclusion.**
  5. **If either premise is particular, any valid conclusion must also be particular** (worded with "Some").
- **Worked example, live:** *"All cats are animals. All animals are living things."*
  1. Statement 1: Cats=✔, Animals=✘.
  2. Statement 2: Animals=✔, Living things=✘.
  3. Common term "Animals" — has a ✘ (statement 1) and a ✔ (statement 2) → **has at least one tick, rule 1 satisfied.**
  4. Connect the two outer (non-common) terms, carrying over their original marks: Cats=✔, Living things=✘.
  5. Match that pattern against the table: ✔✘ = **"All A's are B's."**
  6. **Conclusion: All cats are living things.**

**Checkpoint (at 32 min)** — cold-call:
> *"'No teachers are lazy. All lazy people are unproductive.' Common term is 'lazy' — does it have at least one tick? What does that tell you?"*
> **Answer:** "No teachers are lazy" → Lazy=✔. "All lazy people are unproductive" → Lazy=✔ again. **Yes, it has ticks (both statements' lazy term is ✔) — rule 1 is satisfied, a conclusion is possible.** *(Full conclusion: Teachers=✔ from stmt 1, Unproductive=✘ from stmt 2 → "No teachers are unproductive" — wait, check pattern: ✔✔ pattern = "No A's are B's." So: No teachers are unproductive.)*

## ⚡ ALS Activity 2 — Silent Solve → Vote-Lock → Reveal: Mixed Statement Combo (32–40 min)

**ALS format:** Silent Solve, Vote-Lock, Then Reveal — students combine one particular and one negative statement on their own, applying rule 5 (particular premise forces a particular conclusion) without guidance. Deliberately different register from Activity 1's loud paired race (quiet, individual, single big reveal), and tests a harder rule combination than Teaching Block B's worked example.

**Setup line:**
> *"On your own, two minutes. 'Some doctors are teachers. No teachers are farmers.' Find the tick/cross for each statement, check if a conclusion is possible, and if so, state it. Write your full reasoning, hold it up when I say show."*

Give 2 minutes silent work, then: *"Show me — three, two, one, show."*

**The reveal, step by step:**
1. "Some doctors are teachers" → Doctors=✘, Teachers=✘ (Particular Affirmative).
2. "No teachers are farmers" → Teachers=✔, Farmers=✔ (Universal Negative).
3. Common term "Teachers" has a ✘ (statement 1) and a ✔ (statement 2) → **has at least one tick, rule 1 satisfied.**
4. Not both particular (statement 2 is negative), not both negative (statement 1 is particular) → rules 3 and 4 don't block a conclusion.
5. Statement 1 is particular → **rule 5: the conclusion must be particular.**
6. Connect the outer terms: Doctors=✘, Farmers=✔ → matches the table pattern **✘✔ = "Some A's are not B's."**
7. **Conclusion: Some doctors are not farmers.**

**Debrief line:**
> *"Notice the conclusion had to be 'Some,' not 'All' or 'No' — that's rule 5 doing its job. Whenever one of your two statements is particular, your final answer is locked into being particular too, no matter how the ticks combine."*

**Cut rule:** If running short, cut the silent window to 90 seconds but always show all five rule-checks explicitly in the reveal (common term/tick, both-particular, both-negative, particular-forces-particular) — walking through every rule, even the ones that don't block anything, is the actual discipline being taught.

## Exit Ticket + Homework (45–48 min)

**Exit ticket** (~1 min):

> "All engineers are graduates. Some graduates are managers." Can a definite conclusion be drawn about engineers and managers?
> **Answer:** Engineers=✔✘, Graduates common term has ✘(stmt1) and ✘(stmt2, since "Some graduates are managers" makes Graduates=✘) → common term has **no tick at all** → **rule 1 fails, no conclusion possible.**

Scan responses on the way out — if the "common term needs at least one tick" rule isn't sticking, revisit briefly at the start of Session 22.

## Common Misconceptions

| Misconception | Why students hold it | Correct it live by |
|---|---|---|
| Reversing any statement's wording is always safe ("Some A are B" → "Some B are A" AND "All A are B" → "All B are A") | Only one of these two reversals is actually valid | Teaching Block A's explicit "All→Some reversal is the one safe case" framing |
| The common term just needs to appear in both statements, tick status doesn't matter | Skips rule 1 entirely | Exit ticket's explicit no-tick-at-all failure case |
| A particular + negative combination always blocks a conclusion, like both-particular or both-negative do | Over-generalises rules 3 and 4 to a case they don't cover | Activity 2's explicit worked mixed case, conclusion found successfully |
| The final conclusion's wording ("All," "Some," "No") is chosen freely based on what "sounds right" | Doesn't apply rule 5's forcing logic | Activity 2's explicit "particular premise forces particular conclusion" debrief |
| Tick and Cross replaces the need to understand the Venn method entirely | Sees the shortcut as a full substitute rather than a faster tool for the same logic | Teaching Block A's explicit "same logic, just symbols instead of circles" framing |
